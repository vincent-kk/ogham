# Claims

## A claim predicts; a summary describes

A claim states what must hold, and names what breaks when it does not. If no possible shadow could contradict a sentence, it is summary — it may well be true, but it cannot be checked, and it does not belong in the model.

- Summary: "She cares about quality."
- Claim: "She will hold a release over a known defect even under deadline pressure — a release shipped with a defect she knew about breaks this."

The second half is what makes it attackable. A claim with no "or else" gives you nothing to go looking for.

## Three layers, one per claim

**Shape** — what the thing is: its parts, its boundary, its kind, who or what belongs to it. Refuted by a member no declared part explains, an outsider treated as inside, or one name that turns out to cover two different things.

**Behaviour** — how it acts: what triggers what, what holds while it runs, what it does under pressure. Refuted by a response that arrived without its trigger, a pattern that fails in a second context, or a crisis in which the rule bent.

**Purpose** — what it is for: what it optimizes, what it protects when goods collide. Refuted by a choice that betrayed the declared value, or by something kept alive that the declared purpose would not keep.

Layers fail differently, which is why one claim may not span two: a counterexample against half of it leaves the other half standing, and the claim survives without having been tested. Split it instead.

## Marks

- `seen` — you observed it yourself: read the record, heard the words, watched it happen. Cite where the shadow fell.
- `heard` — a description reached you: a summary, a retelling, someone's account of what they saw. Cite who and when; the original went unchecked.
- `inferred` — it follows from seen or heard shadows, but the thing itself was not observed.
- `assumed` — none of the above. Legitimate, and written down precisely so a later reader can attack it.

The mark binds to the claim's subject, not to your reading act. Reading a design document firsthand makes "the document says X" `seen`; "the system does X" it leaves `heard` at best — the document is its author's shadow of the system, not the system.

There is no mark for knowledge. The ladder tops out at seen-from-independent-angles-and-still-standing, which is not proof; a mark above that would be a claim to have left the cave. An unmarked claim reads as `seen` to whoever comes next — that is the failure this vocabulary exists to prevent: not to grade your confidence, but to keep an assumption from being inherited as a fact.
