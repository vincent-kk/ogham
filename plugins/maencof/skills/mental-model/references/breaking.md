# Breaking a Claim

Attacking a claim means going looking for the shadow that makes it false — not re-reading the shadows that suggested it. Confirmation is cheap and proves nothing: the shadows you already collected are the shadows that produced the claim. The strongest attack is a prediction: if the claim holds, a wall you have not checked must show a certain shadow — name it, then go look. And count your projectors before trusting agreement: three retellings of one account are one shadow, however loudly they agree.

## Where the counterexample hides

**Shape claims** — the one name covering two different things (shadows of two objects, merged into one Form); the member that no declared part explains; the formal chart against who actually gets called; the boundary that is crossed routinely and without ceremony.

**Behaviour claims** — the crisis: routine shows manners, pressure shows the law; the second context, where the pattern quietly fails; the exception everyone routes around and no one mentions; the oldest record you have — subjects drift, and models do not notice on their own.

**Purpose claims** — follow the sacrifices: what was given up names what is protected better than any statement of values; the calendar and the budget against the mission; the thing kept alive that the stated purpose would not keep. When outcomes diverge from the declared purpose persistently, model the outcomes — the declared purpose was somebody's shadow of the subject, not the subject.

## The simulation attack

Take the step-3 scenario and derive the shadows it must have cast if the Form is real — then check walls the claim was not built from: the period before, the audience after, the record nobody cited. A subject that had the chance to cast the predicted shadow and cast a different one is the counterexample; a wall with no light on it is not — name the walls you checked. Prefer this attack when a claim was assembled from tellings rather than events, or when several claims share one story: one walked scenario attacks them all.

## How each medium distorts

Every projector distorts characteristically, and the distortion is itself evidence:

- **Documents** describe the intended thing, and drift stale from the day they are written. A document–reality mismatch is a finding worth keeping: it shows what its keeper wanted to be true.
- **People** explaining WHY they acted cast their least reliable shadow — model from what they did; keep what they said as a shadow of their self-image, which is its own Form worth modeling.
- **Records** survive selectively: what got written down is what someone wanted remembered.
- **Memory** — yours included — smooths toward the plausible. The vault's fixed record outranks recall; that is why the vault is searched first.

And absence: a missing shadow is not a missing thing — perhaps no light fell there. Before a claim leans on "there is no record of it", name the walls you did not check.

## When the attack fails

Say which attack you ran, and downgrade nothing to certainty. "I searched the vault across March–July and found no decision that contradicts it" is a result. "This is what she values" is a different sentence, and you did not earn it.

## When the attack succeeds

The refuted claim stays in the report with its counterexample beside it. A claim someone already killed is more useful than its silent absence, which reads as never-considered.

Then fix the model, not the wording. A claim narrowed until no shadow could contradict it has been retired, not repaired — say that it was retired.
